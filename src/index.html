Understanding global cyber incident patterns

This website presents a curated view of cyber incident data through four analytical dimensions: time, target geography, incident types, and attacker–target relationships. The goal is not to reproduce a complex repository, but to surface the most interpretable structures in the data.

Section 1

How have cyber incidents evolved over time?

More incidents, but not the full picture.

Cyber incidents have increased sharply since 2000 — from fewer than 30 per year before 2010 to nearly 800 in 2024. Noticeable spikes around 2014, 2020, and 2022 align with major world events, including regional conflicts, the COVID-19 pandemic, and state-supported cyber campaigns. But the rise reflects more than raw attack frequency. Organizations detect incidents far more effectively today, the definition of what counts as a cyber incident has broadened, and attribution has attracted far greater institutional attention. The data cannot separate real growth in attacks from better reporting and greater awareness.

Section 2

Who gets hit — and how often?

Concentration at the top is itself a finding.

Cyber targeting is not distributed evenly. The United States alone accounts for a disproportionate share of all recorded incidents — more than four times the next closest country. Russia, Germany, the United Kingdom, and France follow, but at a significant distance. These are not random targets. They are the countries with the largest digital footprints, the deepest institutional presence online, and the most documented attribution efforts. Notably, several of the most targeted countries are also among the most active initiators — a reminder that in cyber conflict, the line between attacker and defender is rarely clean. Section 3

What types of cyber incidents are most common?

What dominates the record is not what dominates the headlines.

Hijacking with misuse dominates the dataset by a wide margin — nearly double the frequency of disruption or data theft, and more than three times that of ransomware. This last point is worth pausing on. Ransomware attracts enormous media and policy attention, yet in state-level incident data it ranks fourth. The gap between what dominates headlines and what dominates the record is not a coincidence: state-sponsored operations tend to favor persistent access and quiet exploitation over the loud, financially motivated attacks that make the news. The chart reflects the priorities of states, not criminals.

Section 4

The same pairs keep appearing.

Cyber conflict has a structure — and it shows.

Some attacker–target pairs appear so frequently in the record that they can no longer be read as isolated incidents. Russia targeting the US, China targeting India — these relationships recur across years, time windows, and incident types. The spikes visible around 2014 and 2022 did not introduce new rivalries; they accelerated existing ones. Conclusion

What the data tells us — and what it doesn't.

Cyber conflict is structured, not random.

Across every dimension in this dataset — time, geography, incident type, and attacker–target pairs — the same pattern emerges: concentration. A handful of countries absorb the majority of recorded incidents. A handful of pairs recur year after year. Hijacking dominates over ransomware. The volume rises sharply after 2014 and again after 2022. These are not noise. They are the shape of cyber conflict as it actually operates.

The record has limits.

What this dataset captures is what was attributed, reported, and recorded — not the full universe of cyber activity. State-sponsored operations that go undetected, incidents that are never disclosed, and attacks where attribution remains contested are all absent. The patterns here are real, but they reflect the priorities of the institutions doing the recording as much as the priorities of the attackers themselves.
